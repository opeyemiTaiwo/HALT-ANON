\begin{abstract}
Hallucination detectors for long-form multimodal generation are almost always
evaluated on text from the same source LLMs they were trained on. We show this
conflates detection with identifying which LLM wrote the text. We release \textsc{halt}
(Hallucination Across LLMs and Techniques), a benchmark of 19{,}361 forensic
video reports (21.8M words) produced by three frontier
multimodal LLMs across eight prompting strategies on 807 surveillance videos,
labelled for six hallucination types spanning fabrication, omission, and
distortion by a cross-judging panel in which no model evaluates its own
output. The corpus is fully crossed: every video is described by every
source LLM under every strategy, so any cross-source comparison holds the
input fixed. Under the standard same-source split, fabrication is the most
detectable axis (AUC 0.89) and distortion the least (0.77). Under
leave-one-source-out the ranking inverts: fabrication falls to 0.686 while
omission holds at 0.793, and binary any-hallucination detection falls from
0.820 to 0.566, near chance. Reference-conditioned features are the only ones
that recover binary detection (0.702), though they underperform on omission.
Because we release per-judge verdicts and independent human labels on a
shared subset, we can further show that the panel's scene-fabrication label
tracks report length (AUC 0.727) where the human label does not (0.566), and
that assigning each source LLM a distinct judge pair, an unavoidable
consequence of no-self-evaluation, confounds source LLM with measurement
instrument. Axis-level signatures survive that confound; one fine-grained
ordering does not. We release the corpus, per-judge verdicts, rubrics,
reliability tiers, splits, and baselines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models are increasingly evaluated by other large language
models. Panel-of-judges protocols now label datasets far larger than any human
annotation effort could reach, and the resulting labels underpin leaderboards,
safety audits, and deployment decisions. Whether those labels measure what they
are taken to measure is rarely examined, and the raw judge verdicts are rarely
released for anyone to check.

Hallucination detection inherits both problems. A detector is trained on text
labelled this way and tested on text from the same LLMs that produced the
training set, split at random. Model families differ sharply in surface style
and equally sharply in how often they hallucinate. A detector with access to
both regularities can score well by recognising which family wrote a passage
and applying that family's base rate, without assessing whether any particular
claim is supported. Under a random split this is invisible, because the models
at test time are the models at training time. The reported accuracy may
therefore describe how distinguishable the writers are rather than how
detectable hallucination is.

The distinction matters wherever a detector is deployed against text from a
model it has not seen, which is the normal case. It matters most in settings
where the cost of an undetected error is borne by someone other than the
system's operator. Forensic reporting is one such setting: the difference
between inventing a detail and omitting a crime is the difference between two
kinds of evidentiary failure, and a screening tool that silently stops working
when the underlying model is updated offers false assurance rather than none.

Separating the two possibilities requires evidence that existing resources
cannot supply. It requires text from several model families over identical
inputs, labelled per instance, so that the writer can be withheld while
everything else is held fixed. Benchmarks built from a single model, or from
several pooled without controlling the split, cannot distinguish a detector
that reads evidence from one that reads style. We therefore ask:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item Do hallucination detectors transfer to source LLMs they were not trained
on, and by how much does performance change?
\item How does detectability depend on hallucination type, and does the type
ordering hold under transfer?
\item Which surface properties carry the detectable signal, and what remains
once source identity is removed?
\item How much of the loss does conditioning on the reference annotation
recover, and for which types?
\item How does panel design shape the labels themselves, both in what drives
judge verdicts and in what excluding self-evaluation implies?
\end{enumerate}

Throughout, we refer to the LLM that produced a report as its \emph{source
LLM}, to distinguish it from the LLMs that later judge it. In the language of
shortcut learning \citep{geirhos2020shortcut}, source identity is a spurious
feature: predictive in distribution, cheaply recoverable from surface style,
and worthless once the source changes. Whether detectors rely on it is an
empirical question, and the remainder of this paper answers it.

\section{Related Work}

Existing hallucination benchmarks target short outputs and a fixed set of
writers. CHAIR \citep{rohrbach2018object} measures object hallucination in
captions, POPE \citep{li2023evaluating} uses polling, HaluEval
\citep{li2023halueval} benchmarks 35K samples, THRONE \citep{kaul2024throne}
unifies evaluation across six models, Bingo \citep{cui2023holistic} identifies
bias triggers, and VideoHallucer \citep{wang2024videohallucer} extends the
setting to short clips. None varies the writing model while holding the input
fixed, so none can separate a detector that reads evidence from one that reads
style. Detection methods inherit the same limitation: SelfCheckGPT
\citep{manakul2023selfcheckgpt}, semantic entropy \citep{kuhn2023semantic}, and
FActScore \citep{min2023factscore} detect hallucination via consistency,
uncertainty, or fact decomposition, but are evaluated within a single writer or
across several pooled without a held-out condition. Our reference-conditioned
baseline is closest to FActScore, simplified to lexical grounding.

Panel designs \citep{zheng2023judging} and cross-model panels that mitigate
self-preference \citep{panickssery2024llm} are now standard, and their
reliability is typically summarised by a single agreement figure. We report it
per hallucination type against independent human raters, and show that the
no-self-evaluation constraint itself induces a source-by-instrument confound
that single-figure reporting hides. The underlying material comes from
UCF-Crime
\citep{sultani2018real}, which provides the video benchmark, and UCA
\citep{yuan2024towards}, whose fine-grained natural-language annotations serve
as the reference; the reports and per-judge verdicts were generated by
prior work \citep{priorwork2026} to measure prevalence, a study that considers
neither detection, transfer, nor the reliability of its own labels.
Section~\ref{sec:corpus} states what is inherited and what is new.

\paragraph{What this paper adds.} A corpus fully crossed over source LLMs and
prompting strategies, released with per-judge verdicts; splits that withhold an
entire source LLM; reference detectors spanning surface, lexical, embedding and
reference-conditioned features; and a diagnosis of the labels themselves.

\subsection{Corpus statistics and label properties}
\label{sec:corpusstats}

Under the released aggregation rule, $91.1\%$ of reports carry at least one
hallucination, with a mean of $2.58$ per report. The rule matters: a
disjunctive rule on the same verdicts gives $98.5\%$ and $3.55$ per report, a
seven-point swing in the headline number that a release of aggregated labels
alone would conceal. Output length differs by a factor of three across source
LLMs under an identical task (Table~\ref{tab:corpus}), which is the regularity
the rest of this section shows detectors exploiting.

Reliability is strongly type-dependent (Table~\ref{tab:reliability}): entity
fabrication, crime misclassification, and phantom actors reach $\kappa$ of
$0.81$, $0.70$, and $0.66$ against the human consensus, while scene
fabrication reaches only $0.15$ and is excluded from the headline macro.
Two qualifications matter for anyone using the resource. The consensus
$\kappa$ is computed only on reports where the two raters agree, and that
subset ranges from 62 to 110 of 130, so H5's $0.81$ rests on 64 reports. And
the raters agree only modestly with each other on the fabrication types
(macro $\kappa = 0.28$, with $\kappa \approx 0$ on H1), below the judges'
mutual agreement. The disagreement has a direction: because reference
annotations are summary-level, a report describing genuinely observed but
granular detail absent from the summary can be scored as fabrication, and on
the consensus subset the panel flags scene fabrication in $62\%$ of reports
both humans call negative while almost never missing a human-confirmed one.
Absolute fabrication prevalence should therefore be read as an upper bound;
the relative quantities the benchmark supports are unaffected, since any
labelling bias is shared across source LLMs.

Judge inputs cap model output at $3{,}000$ characters and $65\%$ of reports
exceed it, with the most verbose source LLMs the most truncated. Truncation can
therefore only understate their fabrication rates rather than manufacture them.

\subsection{Same-source detection reproduces prior findings (RQ1)}

Under the standard video-disjoint random split
(released artifact, \S B--C), fabrication is the most
detectable axis.
Surface features alone reach 0.858 on scene fabrication and 0.865 on entity
fabrication, against 0.646 on crime omission and 0.653 on severity
minimization. TF-IDF improves every type, reaching 0.846 macro and 0.820
binary. This ordering, fabrication detectable and omission resistant, matches
what prior work reports and is what a practitioner would conclude from this
corpus under conventional evaluation.

\subsection{The ordering inverts under source-LLM shift (RQ1--RQ3)}

Leave-one-source-out reverses it (Table~\ref{tab:logo},
Figure~\ref{fig:inversion}). Averaged over
three folds, TF-IDF gives fabrication 0.686 and omission 0.793. Scene
fabrication falls from 0.882 to 0.643 and entity fabrication from 0.888 to
0.671, while crime omission falls only from 0.854 to 0.793 and crime
misclassification from 0.773 to 0.727. The types that looked easiest degrade
four to five times as much as the types that looked hardest.

The separation is statistically unambiguous. Bootstrap 95\% intervals
(Table~\ref{tab:logo}) place crime omission at $[0.776, 0.799]$,
$[0.785, 0.807]$, and $[0.785, 0.806]$ across the three folds, while scene
fabrication never exceeds $0.714$ and entity fabrication never exceeds
$0.730$. The omission interval overlaps neither fabrication interval in any
fold, so each of the two principal fabrication types is significantly less
detectable than omission under source-LLM shift.

The effect is not an artifact of residual video overlap. Because each video
contributes 24 reports, holding out a source LLM leaves the same videos on both
sides of the split. Re-running the three folds with video disjointness
additionally enforced (training on 9{,}003 reports, testing on 1{,}016) lowers
every number and widens the gap: fabrication falls to 0.668 and omission to
0.780, so the omission-minus-fabrication margin grows from 0.107 to 0.112,
and binary detection falls to 0.559. The figures we report throughout are
therefore the conservative ones.

Binary detection collapses outright. Any-hallucination AUC falls from 0.820
to 0.566, with per-fold values of 0.539, 0.548, and 0.613. On the held-out
Claude fold the interval is $[0.502, 0.578]$: detecting whether a long-form
report contains any hallucination at all, on a source LLM the detector has not
seen, is statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip.

The mechanism is visible in the corpus statistics. Scene fabrication
prevalence is 89.6\% for Claude and 70.7\% for Gemini against 30.9\% for GPT,
and mean report length varies from 580 to 1{,}802 words across the three. A
detector that identifies the source LLM from style and applies its base rate
achieves high apparent fabrication accuracy. Removing identity features does
not remove the shortcut, since style is recoverable from the text itself
(released artifact, \S B--C).

Transfer also depends on whether a similar failure profile appears in
training. Holding out GPT, the only omission-dominant family, drops embedding
scene-fabrication AUC to 0.526 while raising crime-omission AUC to 0.824.
Holding out Claude, leaving the similarly fabrication-dominant Gemini, raises
scene fabrication to 0.853. Detectors generalise to a new source LLM mainly
when the training pool contains one that fails the same way, which is an
argument for benchmarks that span multiple source-LLM families rather than
scaling a single one.

\subsection{Grounding recovers binary detection but not omission (RQ4)}

Reference-conditioned features are the only ones that move binary detection
under source-LLM shift, reaching 0.702 against 0.566 for TF-IDF, 0.595 for
embeddings, and 0.583 for surface (Table~\ref{tab:grounded}). They also give
the best fabrication axis, 0.691 when combined with surface features.

They are, however, worse on omission: 0.577 against TF-IDF's 0.793. We
expected the reference-token-coverage feature to capture crime omission
directly and it does not. Whether a report drops the criminal event is
apparently expressed in how the report is written rather than in bag-of-words
coverage of the reference. No feature set wins everywhere, and the best macro
under source-LLM shift is TF-IDF at 0.723, leaving substantial headroom for a
detector that grounds claims in the footage rather than in a lexical
reference. Binary detection under shift is thus the benchmark's open problem,
with omission and distortion under grounding a second target.

\subsection{Panel labels carry a length bias (RQ5)}

Because we release panel labels and independent human labels on a shared
subset of 130 reports, a single feature can be scored against both label
sources (Table~\ref{tab:artifact}). Report length predicts the panel's
scene-fabrication label at AUC 0.727 but the human label at only 0.566, a gap
of 0.161. Severity minimization diverges more sharply still: for humans,
length runs inverse to the label (0.275, well below chance), while the panel
sees no relationship at all (0.505).

Entity fabrication and phantom actors show no such gap, 0.784 versus 0.800
and 0.662 versus 0.668. Panel and humans agree there that longer reports
fabricate more. The bias is therefore specific rather than pervasive, which
is what makes it diagnostic rather than merely deflationary: it identifies
which labels to distrust rather than casting doubt on all of them.

The divergence is specific to verbosity rather than to disagreement in
general. Repeating the comparison for the reference-conditioned features gives
negative gaps on almost every type, reaching $-0.236$ on scene fabrication. On
grounded quantities the panel does not diverge from the human raters at all;
only report length predicts the judge better than it predicts a human reader.

The direction matches the labelling mechanism identified in
Section~\ref{sec:corpusstats}: longer reports contain more granular detail
absent from a summary-level reference, and that detail is scored as
fabrication.


\subsection{No self-evaluation implies a source-by-instrument confound (RQ5)}

Excluding self-evaluation has an unavoidable consequence: each source LLM is
scored by a different pair of judges, and those pairs differ in mutual
agreement by a factor of five on the fabrication types, from Cohen's
$\kappa = 0.850$ down to $0.148$. Under a conjunctive aggregation rule a
disagreeing pair produces fewer positives for purely mechanical reasons, so
aggregated per-source rates confound the source LLM with its measurement
instrument. To our knowledge this has not been reported, and it applies to
any no-self-evaluation panel.

The confound is testable here because each judge scores exactly two
source LLMs, permitting a within-judge comparison that holds the instrument
fixed (released artifact, \S B--C). Across 18 judge-by-type-by-pair
comparisons, the source LLM ordering given by the aggregated labels is
reproduced in 16. At the axis level it is reproduced in all six: every judge
that sees GPT calls it omission-dominant, and every judge that sees Claude or
Gemini calls them fabrication-dominant.

The two failures are informative. Both fall on the GPT judge and both on
fabrication types, where GPT-as-judge flags nearly everything it sees (92.0\%
and 96.1\% on scene fabrication for the two source LLMs it scores) and
therefore cannot discriminate between them. The Claude-versus-Gemini
fabrication ordering rests on a 4 to 7 point within-judge margin and is
judge-dependent. The Claude-versus-GPT and Gemini-versus-GPT orderings, with
within-judge gaps of 29 to 41 points reproduced by every judge, are robust. None of
this is checkable without the raw verdicts, which cost nothing to release and
foreclose nothing: the same verdicts yield $91.1\%$ or $98.5\%$ prevalence
depending on the aggregation rule alone.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Single corpus, three families.} All reports derive from one video
benchmark and one annotation set, and leave-one-source-out has three folds.
The effect is consistent across all three, but three is a small sample for a
claim about source-LLM families in general, and whether the inversion
reproduces on other long-form tasks is untested.

\textbf{Small human subset.} The label-divergence analysis rests on 130
reports, with 62 to 110 per type after restricting to rows where both raters
agree; gaps below roughly 0.10 should be treated as noise, and the two we
report exceed it. Reference annotations are summary-level, which is the source
of the fabrication labelling bias we quantify rather than eliminate.

\textbf{Simple baselines.} All detectors are linear; the comparison of
interest is between splits, using the same detector on both sides.

\section{Conclusion}

Hallucination detectors are evaluated almost exclusively on text from the
source LLMs they were trained on. \textsc{halt} makes the alternative testable,
and much of the reported skill turns out to be recognition of the writer rather
than assessment of the claim: withholding a source LLM inverts the per-type
ordering and drives binary detection to the edge of chance. Grounding against
the reference moves it only part of the way, and the labels carry a related
weakness, tracking report length where human annotators do not. Held-out-source
evaluation should be routine for any detector claimed to detect a phenomenon
rather than a model, and per-judge verdicts released as standard.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

The corpus derives from a publicly available surveillance benchmark of
de-identified footage. Annotation was performed by members of the research
group working from text. We release the resource for auditing and detector
development. It is not suitable for, and we do not endorse, automated legal
or investigative decision-making: a system whose error rate depends on the
source LLM, the prompting strategy, and the event type does not meet the
standard any evidentiary use would require, which is part of what these
results establish.

\section*{Reproducibility Statement}

A single entry point rebuilds every label from the released per-judge
verdicts and regenerates every table and figure, verifying the published
corpus statistics on each run so that divergence is caught immediately. All
splits are seeded. Section~\ref{sec:release} lists the released artifacts and
gives the anonymous repository link.
